Found Family.

A monthly family dinner at a Stanford dining hall.

How might we.

How might we increase social connection between students, their peers, and staff in Stanford dining halls in order to create the family dinner feeling that many students crave while away from home at college?

Team.

  • TH Tess Herrick
  • MG Maggie Goldsmith

02 / Research & Needfinding

The people, the questions, the patterns.

Process.

We ran 1:1 interviews with three students who eat at Stanford in very different ways. A co-op resident, a sorority member, and a freshman in an all-frosh dorm. We also did a 30-minute AEIOU observation during the lunch rush at Arrillaga to watch how the space itself shapes who ends up eating with whom.

Notes.

"I don't tend to take the risk of hoping I run into someone, because what if I don't?"
"At Pi Phi, our chef knows my name. That sounds small but it changes everything."

Findings.

Students' relationship to food is tied to belonging. Nightly family dinners shaped what "home" feels like. At Stanford, students need consistent shared meal times so eating contributes to belonging instead of just nourishment.

Personas.

Persona 01

Sam

A junior from LA who has lived in Columbae for two years. In a co-op where everyone takes shifts cooking for the house, food became the daily proof of mutual care.

"A friend who has a midterm the next morning is baking bread for people just because they want to."

Needs A community of people he can trust to show up for one another.

Persona 02

Annie

A sophomore from Miami living at Pi Phi. Grew up with nightly family dinners and didn't realize how much she relied on that hour until she left for college.

"At Pi Phi, our chef knows my name. That sounds small but it changes everything."

Needs Consistent shared meal times with friends, supported by familiar staff.

Persona 03

Natalie

A freshman from NYC in Soto, all-frosh in Wilbur. Has had her best Stanford conversations over dining-hall meals, and the loneliest moments when she couldn't find anyone to eat with.

"I don't tend to take the risk of hoping I run into someone, because what if I don't?"

Needs A reliable way to consistently share meals so eating doesn't feel isolating.

AEIOU Observation · Arrillaga · May 8 · 12:30–1:00 PM

"Almost no one ate fully alone."

During the lunch rush, eating was almost incidental. People were there to be around other people: meals were the excuse, the room was the point. Solo eaters consistently shielded themselves with laptops, phones, or headphones, suggesting that being alone in a social space feels exposing. People scanned the room before sitting, choosing seats based on who was already there, not what was open.

03 / Synthesis & Ideation

From signals to a shape.

Insight.

We noticed that students' relationship to food and meals are strongly tied to their sense of belonging and connection on Stanford's campus. Based on various interviews, we identified that nightly family dinners shaped ideas of what home feels like. We also concluded that here, students need consistent shared meal times with friends, supported by familiar staff, so that eating at Stanford contributes to belonging. These observations reveal that the dining hall has untapped potential to function as a place of ritual and connection, not just nourishment. This is important because meals happen three times a day, every day, making the dining hall one of the highest-frequency, lowest-effort opportunities Stanford has to foster belonging without asking students to seek it out.

Problem.

Stanford students crave the family dinner feeling that shaped their sense of home growing up. But the dining hall experience is often transactional: swipe in, grab food, eat with whoever you already know or eat alone. The peers and staff who could give students a sense of belonging remain strangers.

Ideation.

We brainstormed five directions before landing on Found Family.

Idea 01

The Community Table

A long table reserved one night a week for students and rotating dining staff to share a meal together. Conversation prompts at each seat to break the ice.

Idea 02

Cooking class with the chefs

A monthly 90-minute evening class at Stern where a dining chef teaches twelve students one of their signature recipes. Everyone cooks alongside, then eats together at the end.

Idea 03

Booklets and posters of staff

Visual storytelling so students see the people behind their meals as more than line workers with names, stories, and the dishes they're known for.

Idea 04

Dinner siblings

A quarterly pairing program: students opt in via short form and get matched into pairs or trios who commit to eating together once a week, ideally across class years.

Idea 05 · Selected ★

Found Family

A monthly themed family dinner at a Stanford dining hall: where each organizer brings five friends who don't know each other, and each of those friends brings one more. Ten-plus strangers, one table, conversation cards in the middle.

Why this one Free to test, easy to iterate, and it hits the insight most directly: making the dining hall feel like a ritual instead of a stop.

04 / Prototype

How Found Family works.

  1. 01

    Organizers create the table

    Three organizers each invite five friends who don't know each other. Each friend brings one more. Ten-plus people, one table.

  2. 02

    A theme draws them in

    Each month has a theme that doubles as a conversation starter. The inaugural dinner's theme: favorites.

  3. 03

    Cards keep it flowing

    Conversation cards sit in the middle of the table. Three escalating tiers: warm-up, get-to-know-you, and the deeper "Found Family" prompts.

  4. 04

    It becomes a ritual

    Once a month at the same hall. Eventually, dining staff are invited too. The goal isn't one good night: it's a monthly place that finally feels like family.

Development.

We designed and sent out a Partiful invite, built out the conversation cards, and gathered theme materials (favorite snacks and desserts collected from each guest in advance).

Iteration.

We pre-tested the conversation cards by having two strangers ask each other the questions we came up with. After each one we asked them how it felt to answer, and we watched closely to see which questions led to a good conversation. The first pass had cards that felt either too small or too heavy. We then rewrote them into three tiers so the table can ease in, then go deeper.

The deck.

Conversation cards.

Tier 01 · Warm-up

What is a food that instantly takes you back to your childhood?
If you could only eat one Stanford dining hall meal for the rest of the quarter, which one would it be?
What's a food opinion you'll die on a hill for?

Tier 02 · Get to know you

What's something you're really into right now?
What's a small ritual you have that makes a regular day feel good?
What's something you used to believe really strongly that you've changed your mind on?
What's the last thing you got really, embarrassingly excited about?
What would your TED talk be about, even if you never actually give it?
What is the wildest thing you genuinely believe is true?
What made you laugh today?

Tier 03 · Found Family

What is a tradition, from your family or one you made up, that you'd want to carry into your future home? Why?
What is something you wish people knew about you sooner when they first meet you?
When was the last time you felt really seen? What did it feel like?
What does "home" feel like to you when you are not thinking of a place?
Who in your life feels like family, even though they're not technically family? What makes you see them that way?

05 / Testing & Next Steps

What we learned.

The session.

We ran the first Found Family dinner at Stern Dining on Wednesday, May 27, 2026 at 6:00 PM. The theme was favorites: each guest's favorite snack and dessert were laid out before the meal as a conversation starter, with the card deck in the middle of the table for when things quieted down.

Where
Stern Dining Hall
When
May 27, 2026 · 6:00 PM
Who
Ten+ students, none in the same friend group
Theme
Favorites · snack & dessert

Feedback.

We collected feedback two ways: a short post-dinner survey from every attendee with specific, meaningful questions about connection and the conversation cards, and two follow-up 1:1 interviews with guests who had different experiences at the table.

Findings.

Six guests filled out the post-dinner survey. The signal was clear: the format worked.

83% would come to another Found Family dinner
7.7 avg connection by end of night (out of 10)
4 of 6 exchanged contact info or said they were hoping to
"People you find that make you feel seen." Answer to: what would you tell a friend Found Family is?

The most honest finding was about the cards themselves. Half of guests rated them "neutral, barely used them," and one more never used them at all. Only one person found them genuinely helpful. The cards weren't what made the night work. The food, the table, and a room of people you hadn't met yet were. The cards belong in the middle of the table as a quiet backup, not the engine.

Next steps.

We're hosting a second Found Family dinner before the quarter ends to test whether the format holds when different organizers seed the table. Longer term, we're proposing it to R&DE as a recurring program, with dining staff invited to the table once it has a track record. The cards will become a smaller, optional deck. The vision: Found Family in every Stanford dining hall, three meals a day, costing R&DE almost nothing.
